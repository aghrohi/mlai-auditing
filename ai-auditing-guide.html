Auditing Artificial Intelligence

A field guide for assuring AI systems — the obligations, the curriculum, and the warning signs.

AI auditing is the independent evaluation of whether an AI system is governed, controlled, compliant, and performing within acceptable risk. Because behaviour is learned from data, can be opaque, and degrades over time, the discipline blends classic audit methodology with data science, model risk management, ethics, security, and a fast-moving regulatory landscape. This page lays out what is required, what you should learn, and what to keep an eye on.

00 Orientation

What AI auditing actually is

An AI audit provides independent, evidence-based assurance over an AI system across its whole lifecycle — data, model, deployment, governance, and monitoring — and recommends improvements. It is not building the model, not marketing it, and not a guarantee of perfection.

Object

Auditing AI

Evaluating AI systems as the subject of assurance: governance, data, model risk, fairness, security, controls.

Tooling

AI in the audit

Using analytics and GenAI to perform audit work faster — which itself raises quality and confidentiality questions.

Stance

Independent & objective

Free from impairing relationships, with a questioning, skeptical mindset and conclusions tied to evidence.

◆ Mental model

Treat an AI system like any high-risk process: there must be a clear accountable owner, a risk assessment, controls proportionate to impact, evidence that those controls work, and monitoring that catches drift. AI just adds new failure modes — bias, drift, opacity, poisoning, hallucination — on top of the usual access, change, and operations risks.

01 Requirements

What is required to audit AI

"Requirements" come from four directions: the laws and regulations that apply to the system, the frameworks and standards you audit against, the professional standards that govern how you audit, and the governance preconditions that must exist for an audit to be meaningful — plus the evidence an audit needs to reach a conclusion.

1.1 Regulatory & legal obligations

These define much of the audit's criteria. The set that applies depends on jurisdiction, sector, and use case — a single high-risk system may be subject to several at once.

InstrumentWhat it requires (audit-relevant)
EU AI ActRisk-based regime with four tiers. Prohibited practices banned (e.g., social scoring); high-risk systems need risk management, data governance, technical documentation, logging, human oversight, transparency, and accuracy/robustness, plus conformity assessment, CE marking and EU-database registration. GPAI models carry transparency + documentation duties (with extra duties for systemic-risk models). High-risk obligations apply from Aug 2026; max fines up to €35M / 7% of global turnover.
NYC LL 144Automated employment decision tools must undergo an independent bias audit within the prior year, publish a summary (selection rates / impact ratios across sex and race-ethnicity), and notify candidates.
GDPR (Art. 22)Restricts solely automated decisions with legal/significant effects; requires safeguards (human intervention, ability to contest) and meaningful information about the logic. DPIAs for high-risk processing.
SR 11-7US banking model risk management guidance: governance, independent model validation, and "effective challenge." Pre-dates modern AI but its principles strongly inform AI model audits.
Sectoral / fair-lendingECOA adverse-action notices with specific reasons (applies to AI-driven credit), and EEOC expectations that anti-discrimination law (Title VII, ADA) applies to AI hiring tools.
US state AI lawsA shifting patchwork (e.g., Colorado's law was enacted, delayed, then repealed-and-replaced in 2026). Most target algorithmic discrimination in consequential decisions via developer/deployer duties — confirm current status before relying on any.
⚠ Volatility warning

AI regulation is moving faster than almost any compliance domain. Effective dates slip, laws get amended or struck down, and new ones appear. Treat any specific date or threshold as perishable, and set audit criteria from the rules actually in force for that entity and location at the time of the engagement.

1.2 Frameworks & standards to audit against

Voluntary frameworks supply structured, recognised criteria. Adopting them builds good practice and a head start on compliance — but does not by itself satisfy a specific law.

NIST AI RMF
Organises AI risk into Govern, Map, Measure, Manage, plus seven trustworthiness characteristics (valid & reliable; safe; secure & resilient; accountable & transparent; explainable & interpretable; privacy-enhanced; fair with harmful bias managed). Voluntary and non-certifiable.
ISO/IEC 42001
First certifiable AI management-system standard (Plan-Do-Check-Act + reference controls). The closest thing to "ISO 27001 for AI."
ISO/IEC 23894
Guidance on AI risk management and integrating it into organisational processes (guidance, not certifiable).
COSO ERM
Enterprise risk framework into which AI risk is best integrated rather than siloed.
OECD / UNESCO
High-level, values-based principles for trustworthy/ethical AI that inform many national regimes.

1.3 Professional & audit standards

These govern how you audit, not the AI itself. Conformance underpins the credibility of your conclusions.

  • IIA Global Internal Audit Standards — mandatory guidance for the professional practice of internal auditing.
  • ISACA ITAF — IT audit & assurance standards, guidelines, and techniques.
  • COBIT — governance and management of enterprise IT; a source of control objectives.
  • Codes of ethics — integrity, objectivity, confidentiality, competency; plus due professional care and a Quality Assurance & Improvement Program.
  • ISACA AAIA (Advanced in AI Audit) — the first advanced, audit-specific AI credential, covering AI governance & risk, AI operations, and AI auditing tools & techniques.

1.4 Governance preconditions

If these are missing, there is little to assure — their absence is itself a finding.

Structure

Three Lines Model

First line owns risk; an independent second line (incl. model validation) oversees; internal audit is the independent third line.

Visibility

AI / model inventory

A catalogue of systems with purpose, owner, and risk tier — the basis for oversight and audit scoping.

Ownership

Accountable owner

A named person answerable for each system's risk, performance, and compliance, with clear RACI.

Policy

AI governance policy

Principles, roles, and requirements for how AI is developed, used, and controlled — and evidence it is followed.

1.5 Evidence the audit needs

An opinion must rest on sufficient (enough) and appropriate (relevant + reliable) evidence. AI evidence is largely digital and version-bound.

  • Documentation — model/system cards, data lineage and datasheets, validation reports, DPIAs, approvals.
  • Logs & audit trails — inputs, outputs, decisions, changes, and access (tied to a specific model/data version).
  • Reproducibility — being able to re-run training/scoring and obtain consistent results.
  • Test results — performance, fairness, robustness, and red-teaming evidence — ideally independently obtained.
02 Curriculum

What to learn

An effective AI auditor is a generalist with real depth in a few areas. You do not need to train models, but you must understand them well enough to evaluate risks and challenge claims. The map below spans the technical, the methodological, and the regulatory.

2.1 Audit foundations & methodology

The bedrock: independence vs objectivity, risk-based planning, the audit lifecycle (plan → fieldwork → report → follow-up), materiality, sampling, the five attributes of a finding (criteria, condition, cause, effect, recommendation), and root-cause analysis.

2.2 AI/ML fundamentals

Enough to be dangerous: supervised vs unsupervised learning, training/validation/test splits, overfitting, and the core metrics.

ConceptWhy an auditor needs it
Precision / recallUnderstand the false-positive vs false-negative trade-off behind a model's decisions and its fairness.
Confusion matrixRead where a model errs, and reason about the differing harms of each error type.
AUC-ROCJudge separability across thresholds — and know its limits versus threshold-specific metrics.
ThresholdsThe decision cut-off changes who is selected/flagged; it must be justified and consistent.
Overfitting / driftRecognise when reported performance won't hold in production.

2.3 Data governance & quality

Models are their data. Learn data lineage and provenance; quality dimensions (accuracy, completeness, consistency, timeliness); labelling quality and ground truth; representativeness and class imbalance; data minimisation, classification, and retention; and the trap of data leakage between train and test sets.

2.4 Model risk & validation

The heart of model assurance: conceptual soundness, independent validation and "effective challenge," benchmarking and challenger models, backtesting and outcomes analysis, sensitivity/stress testing, use-outside-intended-purpose, and revalidation triggers. Validation intensity should scale with the model's risk tier.

2.5 Fairness & bias auditing

The metrics and their tensions: demographic parity, equalised odds, equal opportunity, predictive parity/calibration — and the impossibility of satisfying them all when base rates differ. Plus disparate impact, the four-fifths rule, proxy discrimination, intersectional bias, counterfactual fairness, and why "fairness through unawareness" fails. Treat bias as sociotechnical, and document the chosen definition.

2.6 Transparency & explainability

Global vs local explanations; intrinsic interpretability vs post-hoc methods (SHAP, LIME) and their instability; model and system cards; counterfactual explanations; and disclosure/labelling of AI-generated content.

2.7 Security, privacy & robustness

AI-specific threats — data poisoning, evasion (adversarial inputs), model extraction, inversion, membership inference, backdoors, and prompt injection — plus privacy (DPIA, differential privacy, anonymisation vs pseudonymisation) and references like the OWASP Top 10 for LLMs and MITRE ATLAS.

2.8 MLOps & AI operations

How controls live in the pipeline: model registry, CI/CD with validation gates, reproducible environments, experiment tracking, feature stores, deployment strategies (blue-green/canary), rollback, coordinated versioning of data + code + model, and dev/test/prod separation.

2.9 Generative AI & LLM auditing

The newest and fastest-moving area: hallucination controls and grounding/RAG, prompt and system-prompt management, output moderation, training-data memorisation and PII leakage, agentic tool-use risk, evaluating non-deterministic outputs (multiple runs, success rates), and IP/copyright exposure.

2.10 Frameworks, regulation & tooling

Fluency in the instruments from §1, plus audit analytics: CAATs and full-population testing, anomaly detection, process mining, classic techniques (e.g., Benford's Law), and the risks of using GenAI in the audit itself.

2.11 The human skills

Underrated and decisive: professional skepticism, clear writing, stakeholder communication, the ability to challenge confident technical claims, and the judgement to know when "the demo looked great" is not evidence.

03 Progression

A learning path

One sensible order — adapt to your background. Those from audit add the AI; those from data science add the audit discipline.

Step 01 · Foundation

Audit & governance basics

Independence, the audit lifecycle, the Three Lines Model, risk concepts (inherent vs residual, appetite), and control types. If you're technical, this is your gap.

Step 02 · Literacy

AI/ML and data fundamentals

Models, metrics, training/test discipline, overfitting, and data quality/lineage. If you're an auditor, this is your gap.

Step 03 · Frameworks

NIST AI RMF, ISO/IEC 42001, key regulation

Learn the criteria you'll audit against and the obligations that apply to your sector and region.

Step 04 · Depth

Model risk, fairness, security, MLOps

Go deep where your work concentrates — validation, bias metrics, AI security, and pipeline controls.

Step 05 · Frontier

Generative AI & agentic systems

RAG, prompt management, output moderation, non-determinism, and tool-use risk — the area changing fastest.

Step 06 · Credential

Certify & keep current

Pursue a credential (e.g., CISA/CIA as a base, then ISACA AAIA; IAPP AIGP for governance) and commit to continuing education, because the field will keep moving.

✓ Practical move

Build a small portfolio: take one real (or sample) model and write a mock audit — scope, risk assessment, a few tested controls, a fairness check, and findings with the five attributes. Doing the work once teaches more than any reading list.

04 Vigilance

What to keep an eye on

The recurring red flags, the things that quietly change, and the traps auditors fall into. If you scan for nothing else, scan for these.

4.1 Governance red flags

⚑No accountable owner or model inventory

If no one is answerable and no one can list the AI systems in use, governance is hollow and "shadow AI" is likely thriving.

⚑The builders also approve the model

Missing independence — development, validation, and deployment approval concentrated in one team means no effective challenge.

⚑Policy exists on paper only

An AI policy nobody follows, or risk acceptances made without proper authority or documentation.

4.2 Data & model red flags

  • Unknown provenance — training data of uncertain origin, rights, or quality (including web-scraped data).
  • Data leakage — test data contaminating training, so reported metrics overstate real performance.
  • Aggregate-only metrics — strong overall accuracy hiding poor performance on key subgroups.
  • Use outside intended purpose — a model validated for one population or task applied to another.
  • No revalidation — significant data/model changes with no trigger to re-check soundness.
  • "Passed the benchmark" — a single benchmark or demo treated as proof of safety or fitness.

4.3 Fairness red flags

  • Protected attribute "removed" but proxies remain (zip code, name) and outcomes still diverge.
  • A fairness metric chosen with no justification, or cherry-picked to look favourable.
  • No disaggregated or intersectional analysis; false-positive vs false-negative harms not separated.
  • Fairness checked once at launch and never monitored for drift.

4.4 Security, privacy & robustness

  • No testing for poisoning, evasion, extraction, or prompt injection; safety relies on the model's refusals alone.
  • Sensitive data sent to third-party APIs without controls; PII leakage in outputs untested.
  • Weak access control over training data and pipelines; model weights not protected.
  • No AI incident response plan for security or safety events.

4.5 Generative & agentic AI

⚑Ungrounded generation & no output moderation

LLM answers with no retrieval grounding, citations, or content filtering — hallucination reaches users unchecked.

⚑Over-privileged agents

Tool-using agents that can take consequential actions without least-privilege scoping or human approval — a manipulated agent then has real reach.

⚑Unversioned, unreviewed prompts

System prompts and guardrails changed ad hoc, with no versioning, testing, or access control.

4.6 Operational & monitoring

  • No drift monitoring (data or concept) and no defined thresholds, alerting, or escalation.
  • Uncontrolled retraining — models updated without validation or approval.
  • No rollback capability or staged rollout; changes go straight to all users.
  • Monitoring exists but nobody acts on the alerts.

4.7 The regulatory & vendor watch-list

Things that change underneath you and deserve standing attention:

Reg. timelines
EU AI Act phase-in dates, US state laws (enacted, delayed, repealed, replaced), and sector guidance — re-check before each engagement.
Vendor model updates
Third-party/foundation models can change behaviour without notice; accountability for use stays with the deployer.
Fourth parties
Your vendor's sub-processors and their weaknesses flow through to you.
Shadow AI
Unsanctioned tool use by staff — ungoverned data, security, and compliance exposure.
New attacks
The adversarial landscape evolves; yesterday's defences may not hold, so testing must be continuous.

4.8 Watch yourself: auditor pitfalls

✕ Independence & quality traps

Don't audit work you helped build (self-review). Don't accept assertions without corroboration ("inquiry only" is weak evidence). Don't over-rely on automated audit tools or GenAI without validating them — and never feed sensitive audit data into tools that may expose or retain it. Tie every conclusion to evidence, and disclose scope limitations rather than over-claiming.

05 Synthesis

A working checklist

A compact pass over an AI system. Each item maps to the requirements and watch-list above.

Governance & scope

  • Accountable owner and risk tier assigned; system is in the inventory.
  • Independent validation separate from development.
  • Applicable laws/frameworks identified as criteria.
  • Scope and limitations clearly defined and disclosed.

Data & model

  • Data lineage, provenance, quality, and representativeness evidenced.
  • No train/test leakage; metrics disaggregated by subgroup.
  • Conceptual soundness, validation, and revalidation triggers in place.
  • Documented limitations and intended-use boundaries respected.

Fairness, transparency & security

  • Fairness definition chosen, justified, and monitored for drift.
  • Explanations adequate for high-stakes decisions; model/system cards exist.
  • AI-specific security tested (poisoning, evasion, injection); privacy assessed.

Operations & monitoring

  • MLOps controls: registry, validation gates, versioning, rollback.
  • Drift/performance monitoring with thresholds, alerting, and action.
  • Incident response covers AI security and safety events.
  • Findings written with the five attributes; remediation tracked.
